CHAPTER FIVE: CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS

5.1 Introduction

This chapter presents the conclusion and recommendations of the research on the Smart Solar IoT Monitoring System. It summarises the study, highlights major findings from the experimental results in Chapter Four, draws overall conclusions, identifies the contributions of the work, acknowledges its limitations, and provides practical recommendations alongside suggestions for future research. The chapter closes with final remarks on the significance and potential impact of the study.

5.2 Summary of the Study

Problem addressed:
Traditional solar photovoltaic (PV) monitoring systems rely on manual, infrequent readings taken at hourly or longer intervals. This approach suffers from poor data availability (approximately 8.3% of the temporal resolution of IoT-based monitoring), high energy estimation errors, zero real-time fault detection capability, and response times of up to 3,600 seconds. These limitations make it difficult for solar plant operators to detect faults early, optimise energy yield, and maintain system performance, particularly in regions such as Kumasi, Ghana, where solar energy is increasingly important.

Aim of the research:
The primary aim of this research was to design, implement, and evaluate a Smart Solar IoT Monitoring System that leverages Internet of Things (IoT) sensors, the Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) protocol, machine learning (ML) algorithms, and cloud-based data storage to provide real-time, high-resolution monitoring of solar PV systems. The system was designed to outperform traditional monitoring methods in terms of data availability, fault detection, communication latency, and energy estimation accuracy.

Methodology adopted:
The study adopted a simulation-based methodology using a modular, reproducible software architecture. The system was configured through a central YAML configuration file specifying all simulation, PV, sensor, IoT, database, and machine learning parameters. The methodology comprised the following stages:

1. Solar environment simulation: A physically-based solar environment was generated using the pvlib Python library, which computed clear-sky irradiance (GHI, DNI, DHI) using the Ineichen model, solar position (zenith, azimuth), ambient temperature (sinusoidal daily profile), panel temperature (NOCT model), cloud cover (Beta distribution), dust accumulation (linear degradation), and shading effects. The simulation location was set to Kumasi, Ghana (latitude 7.3775, longitude -3.9779, altitude 300 m), with a start date of 15 June 2024 and a sampling interval of 5 minutes.

2. PV power modelling: A single-diode-equivalent PV model was implemented to convert environmental conditions (irradiance, temperature) into electrical outputs (voltage, current, power, energy), accounting for temperature coefficients, inverter efficiency (96%), and panel area (1.7 m², 400 W nominal).

3. Virtual sensor simulation: Three virtual sensors were modelled: the PZEM-017 (DC voltage and current), the BH1750 (irradiance), and the DS18B20 (temperature). Each sensor added calibrated Gaussian noise to the true values and supported validation against physical limits and failure simulation.

4. IoT communication: An MQTT publisher/subscriber was implemented with a simulated in-memory broker (no external broker required). The communication layer modelled packet loss (2% probability), network latency (50 ms mean, 20 ms std), and message delivery tracking. A device simulator published sensor readings at 5-minute intervals.

5. Database storage: All sensor measurements and communication logs were stored in a SQLite database using SQLAlchemy ORM models, enabling reproducible querying and analysis.

6. Data preprocessing: Raw sensor data was cleaned (interpolation of missing values), feature-engineered (hour of day, rolling averages, lag features), and prepared for ML training.

7. Analytics: Performance metrics (energy yield, capacity factor, performance ratio, efficiency), anomaly detection (rule-based and Isolation Forest), fault detection (overheating, low voltage, underperformance, dust accumulation, partial shading, sensor failure), and statistical analysis (mean, standard deviation, 95% confidence intervals, normality tests) were computed.

8. Machine learning: Four regression models (Linear Regression, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost) were trained for power prediction. Three classification models (Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, SVM) were trained for fault classification. Models were evaluated using MAE, RMSE, R², accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score.

9. Traditional monitoring baseline: A traditional monitoring system was simulated with 60-minute sampling intervals to serve as a comparison baseline.

10. Experiments: Nine environmental scenarios were simulated (normal clear sky, low irradiance, high temperature, cloud variation, dust accumulation, partial shading, sensor failure, communication failure, mixed conditions), each repeated 3 times with different random seeds, yielding 27 experiment runs.

11. Comparison: A quantitative comparison of IoT-based versus traditional monitoring was conducted across monitoring accuracy, data availability, communication performance, fault detection, and energy analysis.

12. Visualisation: Twenty-eight publication-quality figures were generated, along with eleven CSV result tables and a final experiment report.

5.3 Summary of Major Findings

The experimental results from Chapter Four yielded the following major findings:

Performance of the proposed model:
The IoT-based monitoring system demonstrated superior performance across all measured dimensions compared to the traditional monitoring baseline. The system achieved a mean power estimation MAE of 1.23 W (PZEM-017 power sensor), compared to 107.85 W for traditional monitoring, representing a 98.86% improvement in power estimation accuracy. The energy estimation error was reduced to 0.38% (IoT) from substantially higher values (traditional), representing a 99.99% improvement.

The machine learning models for power prediction achieved excellent performance, with all four models attaining R² scores above 0.9995:
- Linear Regression: MAE = 2.01 W, RMSE = 2.52 W, R² = 0.9997
- Random Forest: MAE = 1.99 W, RMSE = 2.78 W, R² = 0.9996
- Gradient Boosting: MAE = 1.99 W, RMSE = 2.74 W, R² = 0.9996
- XGBoost: MAE = 2.24 W, RMSE = 3.12 W, R² = 0.9995

Gradient Boosting achieved the lowest MAE (1.99 W) among all models, while Linear Regression achieved the lowest RMSE (2.52 W). All models demonstrated near-perfect predictive capability, indicating that the selected features (irradiance, temperature, voltage, current, time-of-day, and rolling averages) are highly informative for power prediction.

5.4 Conclusion

Overall conclusion of the research:
This research successfully demonstrated that an IoT-based monitoring system significantly outperforms traditional solar PV monitoring across all evaluated dimensions. The proposed system achieved 12x higher data availability, 72,000x faster response time, 98.86% lower power estimation error, and 100% fault detection capability compared to traditional monitoring. The integration of virtual IoT sensors, MQTT communication, machine learning, and cloud-ready database storage created a comprehensive monitoring solution that addresses the key limitations of traditional approaches.

What the study established:
The study established that:
- IoT-based monitoring at 5-minute intervals provides sufficient temporal resolution for accurate energy estimation and real-time fault detection, whereas traditional 60-minute monitoring is inadequate.
- Machine learning models, particularly Gradient Boosting and Linear Regression, can predict PV power output with extremely high accuracy (R² > 0.9995) using readily available sensor measurements.
- The MQTT protocol, even with simulated packet loss and network delay, provides reliable communication with delivery rates above 88% and average latency below 52 ms.
- SVM is the most effective model for fault classification among the tested algorithms, achieving 74.14% accuracy.
- Environmental scenarios (cloud cover, dust, shading, sensor failure, communication failure) have measurable and distinct impacts on PV system performance, with energy output ranging from 850 Wh (low irradiance) to 2,567 Wh (normal clear sky).
- The mean energy output across all 27 experiment runs was 1,884.59 Wh (95% CI: 1,652.30 to 2,116.88 Wh), with a mean efficiency of 26.76% and a mean capacity factor of 0.197.

5.6 Limitations of the Study

Dataset size:
- The simulation was conducted over relatively short periods (1 day per scenario, 3 days for the main dataset), which may not capture seasonal variations, long-term degradation effects, or rare weather events. Real-world PV systems operate over years, and longer simulation periods would provide more robust statistical results.

Dataset diversity:
- The study used simulated data based on a single location (Kumasi, Ghana). Results may not generalise to other geographic locations with different solar irradiance profiles, climate conditions, or altitudes. The pvlib clear-sky model, while physically based, may not fully capture local atmospheric conditions such as pollution, humidity, or seasonal harmattan dust events.

Computational resources:
- The experiments were conducted on a single machine without GPU acceleration. While the pipeline completed in 28.2 seconds, larger-scale simulations (multiple PV arrays, longer durations, higher sampling frequencies) would require significantly more computational resources.

Model complexity:
- The PV model used a simplified single-diode equivalent circuit approach. More detailed models (double-diode, Sandia array performance model) could provide higher fidelity at the cost of increased complexity. The temperature model used the standard NOCT approach, which does not account for dynamic thermal effects or wind-driven cooling variations in detail.

Generalizability:
- The fault classification models were trained on simulated fault data with predefined thresholds. Real-world faults may present differently, and the classification accuracy (63.79% to 74.14%) suggests room for improvement. The models may not generalise to fault types not represented in the training data.

Availability of real-world data:
- The study relied entirely on simulated data. While the simulation was physically based and calibrated, it cannot fully replicate the complexity of real-world sensor behaviour, communication networks, and environmental conditions. Validation against real-world data from operational PV systems would strengthen the findings.

Experimental constraints:
- The MQTT broker was simulated in-memory rather than using a real broker (e.g., Mosquitto, EMQX). Real network conditions, including congestion, routing, and hardware limitations, may affect communication performance differently.
- The number of repetitions was limited to 3 per scenario (reduced from the configured 5 for computational efficiency). A larger number of repetitions would provide tighter confidence intervals.
- The traditional monitoring baseline used a simple downsampling approach. Real traditional monitoring may involve different practices, instruments, and human factors.

5.7 Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, the following practical recommendations are made:

1. Adopt IoT-based monitoring for solar PV systems: The dramatic improvements in data availability, fault detection, and response time demonstrate that IoT-based monitoring should be preferred over traditional manual monitoring for any solar PV installation of significant size.

2. Use 5-minute or shorter sampling intervals: The 5-minute sampling interval used in this study provided 12x more data than hourly monitoring and enabled real-time fault detection. Operators should adopt this or shorter intervals where feasible.

3. Deploy Gradient Boosting or Linear Regression for power prediction: Both models achieved R² > 0.9996 with MAE below 2.01 W. These models are computationally efficient and suitable for real-time deployment on edge devices.

4. Use SVM for fault classification: SVM achieved the highest accuracy (74.14%) among the tested classifiers and should be preferred for fault detection tasks, though further improvement is needed.

5. Implement MQTT with quality-of-service guarantees: While the simulated MQTT achieved 96% average delivery rate, production deployments should use QoS level 1 or 2 to ensure message delivery, particularly for critical fault alerts.

6. Regularly calibrate sensors: The sensor calibration results showed varying accuracy levels across sensors. Regular calibration of irradiance sensors (BH1750) is particularly important given their higher error rates.

7. Monitor communication health: The communication failure scenario showed packet loss up to 11.46%. Operators should monitor communication metrics (latency, packet loss, delivery rate) and implement alerting when these exceed thresholds.

8. Use the Streamlit dashboard for operational monitoring: The developed dashboard provides an intuitive interface for real-time monitoring and should be deployed alongside the data collection system.

5.8 Suggestions for Future Research

1. Larger and more diverse datasets: Future research should extend the simulation to cover full annual cycles, multiple geographic locations, and varying PV system sizes (residential, commercial, utility-scale) to improve the generalizability of the findings.

2. Alternative algorithms/models: Deep learning approaches (LSTM, GRU, Transformer-based models) could be explored for time-series power prediction and may improve fault classification accuracy beyond the 74.14% achieved by SVM. Ensemble methods and autoML could also be investigated.

3. Real-time implementation: The system should be deployed on physical IoT hardware (ESP32, Raspberry Pi) with real PZEM-017, BH1750, and DS18B20 sensors to validate the simulation results against real-world data.

4. Edge/cloud deployment: Future work should explore edge computing for on-device ML inference (reducing latency and bandwidth) and cloud deployment for centralised monitoring of multiple PV installations. Federated learning could enable model improvement across distributed sites without sharing raw data.

5. Integration with other technologies: The system could be integrated with weather forecasting APIs for predictive energy management, battery storage management systems for optimised energy dispatch, and grid-tied inverter control for smart grid applications.

6. Explainable AI: Given that fault classification accuracy was moderate (63.79% to 74.14%), future research should incorporate explainable AI techniques (SHAP, LIME) to understand model decisions and improve trust in automated fault detection.

7. Multimodal approaches: Combining sensor data with thermal imaging, electroluminescence imaging, or drone-based inspection could provide more comprehensive fault detection and classification.

8. Cross-dataset validation: The models should be validated against publicly available PV datasets (e.g., NREL, PVOutput) to assess cross-dataset generalization and robustness.

9. Optimisation for resource-constrained environments: Future research should investigate model compression, quantisation, and pruning techniques to enable deployment of ML models on resource-constrained IoT devices with limited memory and processing power.

5.9 Final Remarks

This research has demonstrated that IoT-based monitoring, combined with machine learning, offers a transformative approach to solar PV system management. The proposed Smart Solar IoT Monitoring System achieved near-perfect power prediction accuracy (R² > 0.9995), 100% fault detection capability, and response times 72,000 times faster than traditional monitoring. These results provide compelling evidence that the integration of IoT sensors, MQTT communication, and machine learning can significantly enhance the performance, reliability, and maintainability of solar PV systems. As solar energy continues to expand globally, particularly in developing regions such as Sub-Saharan Africa, the adoption of intelligent monitoring systems will be essential for maximising energy yield, reducing downtime, and ensuring the long-term sustainability of solar infrastructure. The framework developed in this study provides a foundation for such adoption, offering a reproducible, configurable, and extensible platform that can be adapted to diverse PV systems and operating environments.
